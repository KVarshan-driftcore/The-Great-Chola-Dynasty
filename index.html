Vijayalaya
Early Inscriptions
c. 848 – 871 CE

The Re-emergence

Vijayalaya Chola, rising from the shadows of Pallava overlordship, seized Thanjavur from the Muttaraiyars and restored the ancient Chola line. He founded a temple to the goddess Niśumbhasūdinī (Durgā) and laid the first stones of an imperial future.

Thanjavur became the new capital. The tiger emblem returned to the banners of the Tamil country.

871 – 907 CE

Aditya I — Kodandarama

Son of Vijayalaya, Aditya I ended Pallava supremacy by defeating Aparajita at the decisive battle of Śrīpurambiyam. He absorbed Tondaimandalam and brought the fertile Kaveri delta fully under Chola control.

He is remembered for building a chain of Śiva temples along the banks of the Kaveri — the first great wave of Chola stone architecture.

907 – 955 CE

Parantaka I — Maduraiyum Kondan

Parantaka I pushed Chola power south to Madurai, temporarily subdued the Pandyas, and briefly held parts of Sri Lanka. His reign saw the first major clash with the Rashtrakutas, culminating in the costly defeat at Takkolam (949 CE).

Despite the setback, he consolidated the core Tamil territories and left a realm ready for the imperial leap.

Brihadīśvara
985 – 1014 CE

Rajaraja the Great

Arulmozhivarman, later Rajaraja I, transformed a regional kingdom into the pre-eminent power of South Asia. He conquered Kerala, northern Sri Lanka, the Maldives, and parts of Karnataka. In 1010 he consecrated the monumental Brihadīśvara Temple at Thanjavur — still one of the greatest architectural achievements of the medieval world.

He ordered a comprehensive land survey, created a deep taxation system, and filled the temple with gold, jewels, and thousands of tonnes of rice from conquered lands.

Rajendra I
Gangaikonda
1014 – 1044 CE

Rajendra Chola — Lord of the Oceans

Rajendra I completed the conquest of Sri Lanka, marched north to the Ganges (earning the title Gangaikonda Chola), founded a new capital at Gangaikonda Cholapuram, and in 1025 launched the only major trans-oceanic naval expedition by an Indian power in the medieval period — striking the Srivijaya empire in present-day Malaysia and Sumatra.

He controlled the Strait of Malacca trade routes and brought Ganges water to consecrate his new city.

Rajadhiraja I
Battlefield
1044 – 1054 CE

Rajadhiraja — The Warrior Emperor

Eldest son of Rajendra, Rajadhiraja spent his reign in almost continuous warfare defending the vast frontiers his father had created. He fought the Western Chalukyas, Pandyas, and Sinhalese forces with relentless energy.

He died in battle at Koppam (1054), still fighting — a rare imperial death on the field that cemented his martial legend.

1070 – 1122 CE

Kulottunga I — The Long Peace

Grandson of Rajendra through his daughter, Kulottunga united the Chola and Eastern Chalukya crowns. His half-century reign emphasised administration, land surveys, and internal stability over constant expansion. Overseas territories gradually slipped away, yet the core empire remained prosperous.

Court poets such as Jayankondar celebrated his victories in the Kalingattuparani. Trade and temple endowment flourished.

Chola Bronzes
10th – 13th Century

Golden Age of Art & Faith

Under the Cholas the lost-wax bronze tradition reached perfection. The iconic Naṭarāja — Śiva as Lord of the Dance — remains one of the most recognisable and spiritually profound images in world art. Monumental granite temples, exquisite stone sculpture, and a flowering of Tamil literature (including the final compilation of the Tirumurai) defined the era.

Temples were not merely places of worship but economic, administrative, and cultural centres of the empire.

The Living Machine

Chola Governance & Economy

The empire rested on sophisticated local self-government: the ūr (general village assembly), sabhā (Brahmin settlements), and nagaram (merchant towns). A dense network of merchant guilds — Ayyāvoḷe 500 and Maṇigrāmam — operated across the Bay of Bengal. Land surveys, precise taxation, and temple-centred administration made the state unusually resilient.

Nearly 10,000 inscriptions still survive — the densest epigraphic record of any pre-modern Indian dynasty.

13th Century

Decline & Eternal Legacy

From the late 12th century the Pandyas of Madurai rose again. Under Maravarman Sundara Pandya and later Maravarman Kulasekara, Chola power contracted. In 1279 Rajendra III, the last imperial Chola, was defeated. The political empire ended — yet the cultural empire never did.

The great temples still stand. The bronzes still dance. The administrative genius and maritime spirit live on in the Tamil world.
